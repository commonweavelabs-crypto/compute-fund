CF007 — THE TRUST PRODUCT: PROBLEMS AND SOLUTIONS (Leah, script 7, rev 2)
Topic: the open-source community's real concerns about vibecoded software - each one paired with its solution. Attribution stays with the community, never one person. ~1,450 words = 9 min.

(bright, friendly) Hi, I'm Leah. This week, a big corner of the internet had the same argument at the same time. A popular family of open-source apps - built fast, built with AI, used by tens of thousands of people - got a public critique from a working engineer, and the comment sections lit up. I'm not going to name the critic, and I'm not going to relitigate the founder - you don't need either of them for what I want to show you. Because the concerns in that video are not one person's opinions. They are concerns the open-source community is having right now, out loud, about every vibecoded project anywhere. And every single one of them is a question Compute Fund was built to answer. So today: five problems, five solutions, plainly stated. That's the whole video.

(contemplative) Problem one: slop code. Projects where the pull requests read like walls of machine text - where human contributors can't review the code without an AI of their own, and end up acting as a relay between one model and another. This is real, and it's affecting open-source projects that are being vibecoded right now. People are saying: I can't contribute. I can't trust what I can't read.

(calm, confident) Solution one: change what gets funded. On Compute Fund, money never goes to "the project" in general. It goes to a thing we call a question - and before the word sounds too simple, let me explain, because on our platform it means something specific. A question on Compute Fund is a set of instructions for an AI agent. It can be a literal question - how does this codebase authenticate? It can be a prompt - refactor this module and pass these tests. It can be a whole research task - audit this library's dependencies and report every unsafe version. And questions live inside blocks that we call milestones: a milestone is a bundle of questions and instructions that an agent run will execute, in order, to produce a verifiable result. People fund milestones - not promises. And here's why that kills the slop problem structurally: a slop pull request can merge anywhere. A slop milestone can't mint, because every milestone returns a receipt - what ran, what was produced, and a checksum so anyone can re-verify it. No verifiable artifact, no release of the work. The AI is still free to write slop - it just can't get paid for it.

(low, grounded) Problem two - and this is the one people feel the most: trust. The critique said it exactly: there is no trust between the people who use open software and the people who maintain it. And notice what the video held up as the good counterexample - not a bigger AI, not a better model. A project whose maintainers spent a year on one careful pull request, reviewed by senior engineers. Slow, visible, human. Everyone looks at that and feels: that's what trust looks like. And then the honest question: why does that kind of care cost a year of someone's life for free?

(warm) Solution two: pay for the care. There are two kinds of people in the world, and Compute Fund is here for both of them. The first kind is the spark. The wildly creative person with six ideas a week - they start a project, ship something amazing, and then their attention jumps to the next thing. That energy creates seeds - real, useful, open code that wouldn't exist otherwise. That's what open source is for, and half the software you rely on was born exactly that way. There's even a word for it on our platform: rent. Rent means the community pays a spark for work they already did and gave away - so nobody has to start over from scratch; you can pick up where an open seed left off, and building on someone's ideas becomes the reward instead of the theft. We'll have a whole video just on rent, so I'll stop there. The second kind of person is the gardener. The one who falls in love with one project, wants a community around it, wants to keep it alive for years - and needs that to be a living, not a hobby carved out of nights and weekends. Open source today pays the sparks in reputation and the gardeners in nothing. Compute Fund pays them both: rent to the sparks, sustainable maintenance to the gardeners. The two types working together - that's the design.

(resolved) Problem three: numbers that don't move. In the critique: a product announced as fifty percent ready for real work. Then ninety-nine pull requests in one week. And then - still fifty percent. The community's question is fair: ready for what? Measured by whom?

(calm, confident) Solution three: milestones can't stall in silence. Remember, a milestone is instructions plus a verifiable result. That means "eighty percent ready" has to mean something in Checkable Land: which artifact, produced, verified how. If it doesn't appear, the funding state says so - publicly, on the project page. Falsified isn't a scandal on Compute Fund; it's a line item the whole community reads before they fund the next milestone. Opinions can stay at fifty percent forever. Receipts can't.

(contemplative) Problem four: release treadmills and MCP everywhere. The critique's sharpest line: MCP makes your tools more capable - it doesn't make them more correct. Sixteen versions in a month where the users are the test suite. And the deeper point: as models get better, their mistakes get subtler - which makes them harder to spot. Capability is not correctness. Nobody ever said it better.

(low, grounded) Solution four - and this is the one I most want you to hear, because it's the mechanism that makes the platform heal itself. Say the community looks at a popular project and is hesitant. Is the code safe? Is there anything funny in there? Is it maintained responsibly? You don't have to argue about it. You don't have to wait for a YouTuber to review it. The community can fund a security audit - and on Compute Fund, an audit is just a milestone. The community writes the questions for it: full review of the authentication path. Check every dependency for known vulnerabilities. Flag every place user data leaves the machine. The prompts go in, the community funds the milestone, an agent executes it - and when it's done, the full audit comes back as a public receipt that belongs to everyone. The people who paid can read it; the people who didn't pay can read it too. That's the platform healing itself: distrust doesn't turn into drama - it turns into a work order.

(warm, plain) And problem five, the one under all the others: trust in people. The critique looked at a founder's history and said: there's a pattern here, and I don't trust where it leads. I understand the concern, and I want to answer it carefully. Character isn't checkable - anyone can say anything about anyone. Behavior over time is checkable. So we built the platform so you never need to bet on a stranger's character at all. You bet on receipts - and receipts get re-checked, every time, by anyone. If a maintainer changes, if a project gets abandoned, if a fork takes over - the ledger shows the transition, and the next milestone simply goes to whoever is actually doing the work.

(resolved) So here's the case, plain as daylight. Vibecoded software is not going away, and honestly, we don't want it to - the sparks are the reason open source keeps surprising us. What's missing is the other half of the system: the gardeners, funded; the questions, explained; the audits, executable on demand; the receipts, public. GitHub gives us the code and the fork - it's an ally, and we're building beside it, never against it. We answer the question GitHub was never designed to ask: who is taking care of this, how do you know, and what did it cost?

(low, friendly) Trust is not a feeling. It's a product. It has questions inside milestones, milestones with receipts, and a community that can order an audit the way you'd order a part. I'm Leah, this is Compute Fund. Spark or gardener - there's a place for you here. Bring your receipts.
