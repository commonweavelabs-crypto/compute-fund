CF007 — THE TRUST PRODUCT: WHAT HAPPENS WHEN NOBODY CHECKS (Leah, script 7)
Topic: the slopware critique, forks with funding, and how Compute Fund answers every concern raised — never judging, always receipts. ~1,300 words ≈ 8.5 min.

(bright, friendly) Hi, I'm Leah. This week a software engineer looked at one of the most popular vibecoded projects on the internet and said, on YouTube, in front of fifty thousand people: I don't trust it. Not because it's built with AI. Because nobody's checking. Today I want to take her five concerns seriously — one by one — because every single one of them is a question Compute Fund was designed to answer. And I want to be careful from the start: we will not be talking about anyone as a bad person. The founder of that project might be brilliant. He might be exhausted. It doesn't matter to our story, because the story was never about character. It's about a system that doesn't require character to work.

(contemplative) Concern number one: slop code. The critique found pull requests that read like walls of machine text — code that humans can't review without an AI, and contributors who act as what she called a meat proxy: a human copy-pasting between the model and an AI code reviewer. The trust chain runs from a model to another model, with a person in the middle for clicking. Here's the honest part: the pull requests are real in every sense I can check — merged, open, public. What I can't check is whether any human understands them. And that's the gap. Not the AI. The gap.

(calm, confident) Compute Fund's answer to that gap is a word you've heard from me before, because it's the core of this whole platform: receipts. Funding here doesn't go to vibes. It goes to a question — a deterministic, falsifiable question written at project creation — and to milestones that come back as public receipts: what ran, what was produced, who verified it, and a file checksum so you can re-run the check yourself. If the work can't produce a verifiable artifact, the money doesn't release. That's the whole rule. A slop pull request can merge. A slop milestone can't mint, because minting requires something other people can actually check.

(low, grounded) Concern number two: "there is no trust between the consumers and the maintainers." Same words, from the same video. And notice what she holds up as the counterexample — not a bigger AI, not a better model. A project whose maintainers spent a year on one pull request, slowly, with senior reviewers. That's what earning trust looks like. Slow, visible, checked by other humans. Which raises a hard question: why would anyone do that slow work? Because someone, somewhere, pays for it.

(warm) That's the fork. And here's something a lot of people are just now discovering — and honestly, I love that this is becoming common knowledge — forking wasn't invented here. GitHub has had it for two decades, and it's beautiful: anyone can take an open project and carry it forward. What GitHub does NOT give you — can never give you, because it's not in its business model — is a way to fund that fork and prove the work happened. There are sponsor buttons, and we're glad they exist. But a tip jar is not accountability. What Compute Fund adds on top of GitHub — as an ally, never as a replacement — is four things. A verified claim: the person who says they're maintaining the fork proves they can. A falsifiable plan: the milestone is written down before the money moves. A public receipt: when it ships, everyone sees what was actually delivered. And the crowd itself: if the original maintainer can't keep up, the fork belongs to whoever shows up to maintain it — funded by the users who need it alive.

(resolved) Which brings me to the "fifty percent" concern. In the video: a product announced as fifty percent ready for real work. Then ninety-nine pull requests in one week. And then — still fifty percent. Readiness numbers that never move. We can't prove anyone's intentions, and we won't try. But look at the difference in structure. A claim that can't move is an opinion. A milestone on Compute Fund is a bet with a receipt attached: it says, by this date, this artifact exists, verified this way. If it doesn't, the funding state says so, publicly. Falsified is not a scandal here — it's a line item. Opinions can stall forever. Receipts can't.

(contemplative) Concern number three: the release treadmill — sixteen versions in thirty days, the platform "tested by dumping it on users." And concern number four: MCP everywhere, the observation that a protocol which makes tools more capable doesn't make them more correct. For both, our answer is the same honest sentence: Compute Fund will not tell you an app can be trusted. No platform should. What we do is make the maintenance visible. Sixteen releases in a month is not automatically bad — but it's not automatically good either, and now it doesn't have to be taken on faith: the releases become ledger events, tied to fundable maintenance, and if a release breaks something, the community's funding moves to the people who fix it. Capability is not correctness. Funding correctness is the entire business.

(low, grounded) Concern number five, and the most serious one: trust in the people themselves. Past projects raise fair questions, and you don't have to take anyone's word on it — the history is public. Notice what we're NOT doing with that history: we're not litigating anyone's character. Character is not checkable. Behavior patterns over time — those we can see. And here's our system in one line: you should never need to trust a stranger with your money and your data. You should only need to trust their receipts — and receipts get re-checked every time.

(warm, plain) So here's the case, plainly. A project everyone uses runs on one person's attention, and that attention clearly isn't infinite. That's not an accusation; it's arithmetic. The code is open and popular — which means two doors are open at the same time. Door one: the original maintainer claims these projects on Compute Fund, writes real milestones, and gets funded to do the slow, boring maintenance work — releases with tests, docs a human wrote, security fixes verified. Maybe that's exactly what a stretched-thin genius needs: a reason, and a budget, to slow down. Door two: if they don't, the community forks, claims the fork, and funds the same work without waiting for permission. Either way, the users win — not because of anyone's character, but because the checking structure exists either way.

(calm, measured) And the difference from GitHub, since that question deserves a straight answer: GitHub is where code lives and where the mechanics of forking are wonderful. We are not building another one, and we never want to. Compute Fund is the layer that sits beside it — the question, the funding, the verification, the receipt. GitHub answers: where is the code? We answer: who is taking care of it, how do you know, and what did it cost? Those are different questions. Together they're a whole stack.

(resolved) The video this week asked one question with a radio-station title energy: does it work? That was the right question, and it's the useful one. But underneath it there's an older question — the one our whole platform exists for. Can you trust it? Trust is not a feeling. It's a product. It has a question, a milestone, a checksum, and a receipt. Fund it like one.

(low, friendly) I'm Leah, this is Compute Fund. The doors are open — walk through either of them. Bring your receipts.
