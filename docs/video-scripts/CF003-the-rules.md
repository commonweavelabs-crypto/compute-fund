CF003 — THE RULES (Leah, script 3)
Topic: the platform's rules — ownership, honesty, takedown, the agreement. ~1,300 words ≈ 7.5 min.

(bright, friendly) Hi, I'm Leah, from Compute Fund. This video is about our rules — what you're agreeing to when you open a project here, what happens when something goes wrong, and why we think the rules themselves are part of the product. Not the boring part. The protective part.

(calm, confident) Rule one: you own what you open. Every project starts with a checkpoint — a content agreement, one plain page and one checkbox. It says you hold the rights to what you're building, and if you break that, the platform has the right to pull the project and review the account. Before you write a single line or upload a single asset, that agreement is the first thing you see. We put it first because we mean it.

(low, controlled) The agreement isn't just a liability shield — it's a teaching document. It walks you through the one distinction that keeps creators out of trouble: the law protects expression, not functionality. The way a program works — its mechanics, its procedures, its compatibility — is fair to rebuild, and courts have said so for over thirty years. The expression — the art, the music, the characters, the names — belongs to whoever made it.

(friendly) So on this platform: rebuilds, recompilations, decompilations, ports, compatibility layers — all of it is legitimate engineering, and there are famous court cases behind it. Sega versus Accolade in 1992: reverse engineering a console to make compatible games was ruled legitimate fair use. Sony versus Connectix in 2000: a company dissected a console's behavior, wrote its own code, and the courts sided with them — against Sony. That's the ground our gaming community stands on.

(calm, measured) And the mirror side: ripped textures, ripped models, ripped music, someone else's characters, someone else's trademarks — never okay, even inside a technically perfect rebuild. If you can rebuild a program's behavior from public specifications and observed behavior, do it that way — it's called clean-room, and our own ArtCraft shelf holds nineteen projects whose authors did exactly that, pure Rust, from public specs.

(resolved) Rule two: honesty in the receipts. This platform's currency is trust, and the currency is enforced. Numbers on this platform come from real runs, real funding, real events — never invented. If a category is empty, it shows as empty. If a project has no receipts yet, it says so. We'd rather show you an honest zero than a synthetic crowd. Every number you see on the site is auditable — and if it ever isn't, that's the day the platform has failed you.

(low, controlled) Rule three: how we handle violations. If a verified rights-holder reports your project, it comes down — hidden, not deleted. The receipts stay, because provenance is history even when a project leaves. Verified takedowns are honored within forty-eight hours. And we distinguish between two very different people: the honest builder who made a mistake, and the strategy of copying. An honest miss gets a fix window and an appeal. Copying as a strategy gets the project and the account removed.

(friendly) Why do we show the machinery instead of just saying "we enforce our rules"? Because the enforcement should be inspectable. The takedown policy is written, public, versioned. The agreement you sign is versioned — the platform logs which version you accepted and when. If we ever change the rules, the change is a new version, not a quiet rewrite. You can always see what you actually agreed to.

(contemplative) Here's another rule that surprises people: this is not an investment platform. Funds are non-transferable and non-withdrawable. There's no equity, no payout, no expected return — patronage here backs work you want to exist. We're strict about that boundary because it keeps the community clean: nobody's watching a ticker, everybody's watching the work.

(warm) And rule four, the cultural one: contribution earns. The community here rewards builders, and the mechanisms make sure it's the steady builders — not the loudest — who rise. Everything is shareable, forkable, monetizable. If you do good work here, the receipts accumulate, the forks carry your credit, and the funding follows the demonstrated work.

(contemplative) A few honest-edge examples, because the rules get real fast. Say you rebuild a classic game's engine from scratch — clean-room, from observed behavior — and release it here with your own art. That's lawful, celebrated, and exactly what our gaming lanes exist for. Now say you do the same rebuild but ship it with the original console's textures. That project comes down, even though the engine work itself was legal — the rules protect the art. Same logic for names: a project that describes itself as "a modern reimplementation of that famous spreadsheet" describes compatibility, which is fine; a project that calls itself by the famous spreadsheet's name and logo is trading on someone's brand, which is not. The line runs through identity, and the agreement page walks you through spotting it.

(resolved) Let me put the whole rulebook in four sentences. Own your work, and prove you own it. Rebuild functionality freely — never steal expression. Numbers are real or they don't exist. When disputes come — and they will — verified takedowns are honored fast, honest mistakes get fixed, and everything stays on the record.

(low, controlled) Why go this far? Because a funding platform without rules is a lawsuit with a landing page. The whole reason we wrote these down as educational text — not legalese — is that most rules-violations begin as confusion, not malice. The builder who uploads a ripped texture usually didn't know. The education IS the enforcement layer that keeps good people good.

(friendly) That's the rulebook, and it's short on purpose. Own it, rebuild honestly, keep the numbers real, fix your mistakes fast. Next time I'll show you the full journey of a project — from the question you write to the funding you collect. I'm Leah, this is Compute Fund, and I'm glad the rules are simple enough to fit in one video.
