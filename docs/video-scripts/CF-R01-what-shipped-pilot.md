CF-R01 — "WHAT SHIPPED this week" (Leah, roundup segment, segment pilot)
Format: recurring weekly segment (~500-600 words = 4-5 min): 3-4 real open-source releases, each told in one breath with its Compute Fund connection. Sources = our own vetted link-archive for the week; no hype, primary-sourced facts only.

(bright, friendly) Hi, I'm Leah, and this is What Shipped - your weekly scan of the open-source releases that actually matter. Three stories this week, each one with a twist you'll only hear here.

(calm, confident) First, Unsloth. If you've ever wanted to fine-tune an open model but the training scripts scared you off - Unsloth just removed the last excuse. Their new Studio brings no-code fine-tuning to five hundred open models, running free in Google Colab. You describe your task, you pick a model, and you click train. Fine-tuning is the single biggest compute-burn in the open-source world right now. It's what the community actually spends its tokens on. And Unsloth just made it a checkbox. Eighty thousand GitHub stars, Apache licensed, shipping daily. And here's the twist: Unsloth is listable on Compute Fund today, unclaimed - meaning its maintainer can claim it, and the community can fund the very tool their own fine-tuning runs on. The tool that burns the compute, funded by the ones who light the fire.

(contemplative) Second, Samsung just showed us a model so small it's almost rude. It's called LittleBit - quantization down to a tenth of a bit per weight. The result - a thirteen-billion-parameter model, compressed to under one gigabyte. Thirty-one times smaller. That's a frontier-class brain squeezed into a file that fits on a phone. The papers are published, the method is open research, and the official code ships without a proper open license. It's exactly the kind of project that sits at our gate door saying, "I could be funded - once someone spends ten minutes adding a license file." Watch that one. Once it flips open, it becomes claimable, and edge computing becomes the story of the year.

(low, grounded) Third, the radio. An engineering channel had Claude - the AI - design a complete AM and FM radio from discrete transistors. Not a simulation. A real board, ordered from a fab, built by hand, powered on cold, on camera. The result? It turns on. It draws clean power. FM works across half the band, and the first thing that played through it was a Spanish radio ad. The AM section - on camera, honestly - does not work yet. And I want to highlight that last part, because it's the part that made me smile. They showed the failure. An honest spec sheet with a broken AM section is worth more than a shiny demo, because failure in the open is how hardware gets fixed in the open. Their project files are public already. On our side, that's a claimable shape waiting for its license file and its gardener.

(warm) So that's the pattern I want you to see this week - fine-tuning becoming no-code, models becoming pocket-sized, and hardware designed by AI with humans holding the probe. Every story this week produced something open. And every one of them - the tool, the model, the board - is a claim away from being funded. That's What Shipped. I'm Leah - see you next week, and bring your receipts.
