CF004 — HOW IT WORKS: FROM QUESTION TO FUNDED (Leah, script 4)
Topic: the full project journey — the question standard, the wizard, the gateway, receipts, discovery. ~1,400 words ≈ 8 min.

(bright, friendly) Hi, I'm Leah. This is Compute Fund, and today I'm walking you through the full journey of a project — from the sentence you write when you open it, to the funded, receipted, inspectable project that strangers trust. This is the mechanics video. Everything else we've covered hangs off this.

(calm, confident) Step one: the question. When you open a project here, before asking for a dime, you write down what you're actually trying to demonstrate — in one sentence that can be proven right or wrong. "Does this recipe produce a working port on modern hardware?" "Can this tool ingest our archive format and return clean data?" "Does this model route tasks correctly at this latency?" We call it a question because it forces clarity. A project that can't state its question doesn't know what it's building yet.

(friendly) That sentence isn't bureaucracy — it's your north star and your pitch. Patrons read the question and decide in ten seconds whether they're in or out. It also becomes the anchor for the receipts: every run of compute later gets measured against what you said you'd probe. The question standard is the platform's core discipline, and it's machine-checked — the create flow validates the fields, so nobody sneaks in a vague wish disguised as a project.

(low, controlled) Step two: the wizard. The content agreement comes first — the checkpoint you've heard me describe. Then the form: your question, your name, your one-liner. What gets delivered — the artifact a stranger can download and inspect. What would prove you wrong. Your budget — how much funding you're actually seeking. And where the work lives: your public repo, so the world can watch the source evolve in the open.

(warm) Step three: discovery mode. Your project page goes live — the question at the top, the story of why it matters, the roadmap, the receipts panel (empty at first, honestly empty), the category tags, and a claim state. A whole shelf of projects on this platform sits unclaimed — verified imports waiting for their builder. If one of them is your project, you prove you're the maintainer — repo proof — and ownership transfers to you with an ops receipt in the ledger.

(resolved) Step four: funding. Patrons arrive — some from the homepage's live boards, some from search, some because the category interests them. When they fund your project, the money doesn't sit idle or vanish into your Venmo; it becomes budget attached to your project. Capped at the funded amount. No spending on anything except the work. No compute outside the platform's allowed models. Patronage, converted directly into fuel.

(calm, measured) Step five: the runs. You spend the budget on real compute through the platform gateway. Every single run writes a receipt the moment it happens: which model, how many tokens in and out, what it cost in dollars, and what came out of it. The receipts attach to your project page, publicly. This is the part I want you to internalize: the funding trail and the work trail are the same trail. Nobody has to take your word for how the money was spent.

(contemplative) Why go this far with the receipts? Because it converts the two hardest things in crowdfunding — trust and momentum — into mechanical facts. Trust: a patron can read every receipt before funding. Momentum: a creator with thirty receipts and a rising curve can show traction without a single word of self-promotion. The receipts do the talking, in a language that can't be faked.

(low, controlled) Step six: the showcase. Funded, receipt-rich projects feed the discovery boards — what's buzzing, what the community is following, the most funded this month. All computed from real events with real weights. And deliberately non-addictive: no infinite scroll engineering, no slot-machine rhythms. We show you what happened this week; the refresh button works; that's it. Discovering work here should feel like reading a good index, not pulling a lever.

(friendly) Step seven: the fork moment. If your project stalls — if the budget's spent and the momentum's gone — the platform can mark it ready for forking, and we covered what that means: your base becomes adoptable, credited, and rent-paying back to you. Quietly parked work doesn't die here. It recruits its own successors, and you keep a slice of what they earn.

(warm) And step eight, the one that loops forever: proven work attracts the next patron, the next fork, the next question. The ledger grows. The commons compounds. Projects that keep receipts build reputations that outlive any single launch — your track record as a creator here isn't a bio line, it's a public, auditable history.

(calm, confident) Let me zip through the whole life cycle again, compressed: one provable question; the agreement checkpoint; the wizard captures the artifact, the proof-condition, the budget, the repo; the page goes public; patrons fund; the budget becomes capped, governed compute; every run posts a public receipt; the boards reflect real events; stalled bases fork honestly and keep earning; and the loop repeats.

(contemplative) And because numbers make it real: the boards are built from a real event stream — funding weighs ten times a follow, a follow three times a view — all computed live, none of it stored as fake momentum. A project with three receipts in its first week shows three receipts. A category with one qualifying project doesn't get a trending row; the row just doesn't exist until honesty can fill it. We call those honest empties, and they're a feature: the day you see a full board, you know every row on it was earned by real work and real money.

(friendly) There's one more layer I'll mention and cover properly in another video: the community rails — how following, funding, and governance flags shape what rises here without letting the loudest voices rule. But if you only remember one sentence from today, make it this one: on Compute Fund, a project is a question, funded by the crowd, answered by governed compute, proven by public receipts. I'm Leah. Walk the catalog, read some receipts — they're stranger's work, fully inspectable. See you in the next one.
