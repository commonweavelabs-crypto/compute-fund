CF006 — THE ROADMAP: HOW WE STARTED, WHERE WE'RE GOING (Leah, script 6)
Topic: origin + current phase + next milestones, told straight from the roadmap. ~1,350 words ≈ 8 min.

(bright, friendly) Hi, I'm Leah. Platforms love to tell you their vision but skip their history. Today I'm doing it the other way around — the actual story of how Compute Fund started, the phase it's in right now, and the exact milestones ahead. Straight from our own roadmap, no polish.

(contemplative) The origin is simple, and it's not a pitch: open source needed a funding layer, and nobody had built one that was honest about the money. The founding insight was to stop treating funding and computing as separate problems. Patrons don't actually want to hand cash to a stranger and hope; creators don't actually want donations, they want the compute to do the work. So we designed the platform around the wire between those two: money in from patrons, becomes governed compute for builders, comes back out as public receipts. The roadmap document calls those the three rails, and everything on the platform hangs off them.

(calm, confident) The beginning wasn't glamorous. Phase zero was a single static page — four seed projects, funding tiers, two themes. Its job was to answer one question: does this idea hold up on a screen? The receipts schema we wrote for that little page was already the production schema. That was the discipline from day one: no throwaway work. Every prototype was written as if it were the real system.

(low, controlled) Phase one is where we are now, and the roadmap defines it with almost aggressive clarity: the smallest legal loop that moves compute. Not a big bang launch — a loop. One stranger signs up. Funds a real project with a real card. The platform dispenses real, capped compute against that project's question. The receipts post publicly. The roadmap says, and I quote, nothing else counts.

(friendly) Around that loop, phase one added the surfaces you can see right now: project pages written and owned by their creators — hero, story, roadmap, funding state, and that claim state where maintainers prove ownership of imported projects. The homepage became a discovery surface worth visiting. And the legal spine went in the ground: the content agreement as the first screen when you open a project, the question standard enforced at creation, and the ToS stack that keeps patronage clean of investment framing.

(warm) Then came the scale test. The catalog you browse today — more than eighteen hundred projects across productivity, big tech open source, robotics, 3D, tools, gaming, the public-domain commons, agent research, audio, education, home, finance — that's the result of a few very intense weeks of sourcing, license verification, and import. Hundreds of projects imported in runs. Every one with a checked license. Unclaimed ones left as open doors for their builders to arrive and claim.

(contemplative) Two community mechanisms matter enough to name here, because they're on the roadmap and they'll change how the platform feels. First: the fork law — which you've heard me explain in its own video — turning stalled bases into earning seeds. Second: the showcase layer — boards computed from real events, honest empties when a category hasn't earned a row. If the roadmap has a motto for its community surfaces, it's: real numbers or no numbers.

(resolved) So where are we going? The roadmap's next gates are concrete. Finishing the money rails end to end: live payment rails so patronage stops running in test mode, and the compute gateway so budget becomes keys, caps, and model allowlists automatically. Full question-standard linting at creation. The accounts and profile layer — creators and organizations, with organizations designed to be transferable, not owned, so a project can outlive any single person.

(calm, measured) Then phase two widens the community: comments attached to releases rather than noise, ratings, community badges. The Open Gates board — the mismatch feed that shows demand-proven, delivery-starved work, so builders can pick targets the crowd actually wants. And eventually the governance layer — flags with decaying weight, cost pressure instead of exile — the anti-oligarchy design I described in the community video.

(friendly) How will we know we've won? The roadmap has a definition for that too, and it's wonderfully boring: one stranger signs up, funds a project, the platform dispenses capped compute, the receipts post publicly, and the loop repeats with the next stranger. Not a press launch. A working loop. Everything on the roadmap is in service of making that loop boring and repeatable — that's how you build infrastructure.

(warm) What you're watching right now is part of that same phase: an education layer — videos explaining the platform to the people who'll build on it. And the reason I can tell you this roadmap so precisely is the reason you should trust it: it's all written down, in public docs, versioned — the receipts philosophy applies to the roadmap itself.

(resolved) So that's our story. A one-page prototype that refused to write throwaway code. A loop defined so sharply that everything else counts as noise. Eighteen hundred projects deep. And a straight line from here to working payment rails, a live gateway, a linted question standard, and a community that governs itself with pressure instead of bans.

(contemplative) The roadmap also says what we're deliberately NOT doing — and that tells you as much as the features. No token sales, no venture sprint to a launch date, no engagement-farming feeds. New mechanisms get phase-gated: the minimum now is charter, claims, receipts, and the ledger — everything else waits its turn behind working rails. That complexity firewall exists because a funding platform with ten half-built features is worth nothing; one with a fully working money-to-compute-to-receipts loop is worth everything.

(low, controlled) If you want to bet your weekends on a platform, bet on one whose history you can read and whose next steps are published. I'm Leah, this is Compute Fund. The roads ahead are already mapped — come walk them with us.
