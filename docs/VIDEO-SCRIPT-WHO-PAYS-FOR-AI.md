# WHO PAYS FOR AI?
### A Compute Fund documentary script — built ON the Basic Documentary Template
*Draft v1 — 2026-10-25 · narrator-only read · runtime ≈ 16–18 min (~2,900
words) · every quote sourced with speaker + date + the receipt file in the
appendix. The question Gui commissioned: "somebody is already paying for
AI — who, and how much?" — and our take: who pays decides what AI does.
[BEAT] marks = template beats (T1–T10). [ON SCREEN] = visual notes.*

---

[BEAT T1 · THE MIRROR QUESTION · 0:00]

Do you love AI — or do you hate it?

There's no neutral way to answer that in 2026. Ask online, and half the room
will tell you we're on the edge of a new age of discovery, where the cure for
your family's disease is one training run away. The other half will tell you
it's an environmental catastrophe wearing a chat window.

And here's the thing — if you're somewhere in the middle... uncertain,
uneasy, building something with AI at 2 a.m. while also wondering what it's
doing to the world... you're in the biggest company on Earth. That's most
of us.

This video isn't here to move you from one camp to the other. It's here to
put a single question in your hands — the one question underneath all the
arguing — and then follow where it actually leads.

[BEAT T2 · THE PAYABLE-STAKES QUESTION · 0:55]

The people at PBS asked a version of it this month, on one of the most
trusted science channels on YouTube. Their words:

"[QUOTE] Whatever you think about AI, somebody is already paying for it.
Who — and how much?"
— PBS Be Smart, "The Real Cost of AI," October 2026

That question sounds like it's about electricity. It's not. It's about
power. Because whoever pays for AI doesn't just cover the bill —

[ON SCREEN: the question types itself: WHO PAYS FOR AI?]

—they decide what it gets used for. They decide which problems get
attacked, and which ones wait. The discoveries of a lifetime don't happen
by accident. They happen where the compute points.

So today we're going to follow the money in AI — all the way down — and
then we're going to follow something else: the alternative that already
exists, and what it would change about the future of... basically
everything that gets built.

[BEAT T3 · THE HISTORY INOCULATION · 2:10]

Let's start with a fact that should lower everybody's heart rate.

People have been afraid of new machines eating the world for as long as
there have been new machines. When the steam engine arrived, people feared
it. When the calculator came for the mathematician's job, people feared it.
Every generation of computing — vacuum tubes, mainframes, the PC, the web —
came with the same argument: this one will cost too much, take too much,
change too much.

A lot of those fears were wrong. Some were right. Most were expensive.

So when we look at AI's costs today, we're not the first people to stare
at a machine and wonder who's going to pay. We're just the first people
staring at *this* machine.

[BEAT T4 · THE RULE AND THE BREAK · 3:20]

Now here's how the machine is paid for right now.

If you use AI today, you're most likely one of two customers. Either you're
on a ten-dollar plan. A twenty-dollar plan. Maybe free, if you don't mind
the queue. Small metered tokens in exchange for access to someone else's
supercomputer.

And there's nothing wrong with a twenty-dollar plan — for what a
twenty-dollar plan buys. But understand what it is: it's a *subscription
to someone else's priority*. Your question gets as much compute as their
pricing tier allows. The queue you stand in is their queue. The frontier
problems — the ones that need ten thousand coordinated agents running for
days — were never priced for your ten dollars.

Sam Altman said it himself, in his blog, in January 2025:

"[QUOTE] The cost to use a given level of AI falls about 10x every 12
months, and lower prices lead to much more use... the price per token
dropped about 150x."
— Sam Altman, "Three Observations," OpenAI CEO, January 2025

Fifteen-hundred-x cheaper tokens, leading to much more use.

The cost of intelligence is collapsing.

So the question that actually shapes the future isn't "will AI get
cheaper." It's gotten cheap, and it's getting cheaper. The question is:
*cheap for whom? And pointing where?*

Because when a resource gets this cheap, whoever holds the big pools of it
doesn't just save money. They acquire *aim*.

[BEAT T5 · THE STORY-CARRIED CASE STUDY · 5:00]

Let me tell you a story from this September. It's real, it's documented,
and everything I'm about to say has sources.

Tristan Buckmaster is a professor of mathematics at New York University —
one of the best fluid-dynamicists alive. Levent Alpöge is a mathematician
at Harvard, who worked at Anthropic. For over a year, in secret, the two of
them — plus the mathematician Córdoba and collaborators, building on a
decade of prior work — had been closing in on the equations that describe
fluids: Navier-Stokes, the Millennium Prize problem. A million-dollar
question. In the words Alpöge wrote to Buckmaster* — in an email, in
September 2025, a year before any of this broke —

"[QUOTE] ...corporations [would] claim the deepest pure math work."
— Levent Alpöge, email to Tristan Buckmaster, September 19, 2025

He was afraid the biggest labs would take the crown for work done by
mathematicians.

On September 7th, 2026, Buckmaster published a statement. Twelve hours
later, OpenAI announced something staggering: their unreleased internal
model — a swarm of about ten thousand coordinating agents — had produced a
proof for that exact problem family. Eighty-eight hours of work. A Lean
formalization. Reportedly something like twenty-two million dollars of
compute.

And the dispute that followed tells you everything about who pays and who
owns. Buckmaster said his and Alpöge's recent Advances — from August 15th —
were leaked to OpenAI days before, and may have seeded the whole run.
OpenAI first said they saw none of it "through any means." Then — a widely
documented back-and-forth later — they acknowledged their system had used
session data from the duo as "de-identified, derived" training material.
The offers they made Buckmaster included rewriting their proof as sole
author — with the million-dollar Clay prize going to the mathematicians as
"the closest humans to the problem" — while the method and the model stayed
internal. He said no. A group eventually grew to twenty-eight Fields
Medalists signing a declaration titled "A Severe Misalignment of AI in
Mathematics."

[ON SCREEN: the timeline of the assertions — they changed.]

Now — I want to be careful with this story. There's no court ruling here.
There's a public dispute, competing accounts, and a company that changed
its story between the first press release and the follow-up. Nobody has
been found to have done anything illegal. Maybe they didn't.

But look at the *shape* of it. A year of human work. A leak. A hundred-
million-dollar-scale compute run at the end. And then... whose name goes
on the discovery? Who gets the credit? Who gets anything?

That's what "somebody is already paying for AI" looks like when it's a
discovery rather than an electric bill. The people pay in years of work.
The company pays in compute. And the credit...

...negotiates itself however the bigger table wants.

[PAUSE]

[BEAT T6 · THE QUEST CHAIN · 9:45 — each question mints the next]

So: if code is nearly free — if intelligence is getting 10x cheaper every
year — what's actually left for the people who build?

Laurie Voss is someone worth listening to on this, because he co-founded
npm, the registry that distributes most of the world's open-source code.
At the AI Engineer conference, in October 2026, he put up a number:

"[QUOTE] Developers who switch on autonomous coding agents write 741% more
code — eight times as much. But they ship only 30% faster."
— Laurie Voss, co-founder of npm, October 2026

Eight times more code. A third more shipping. That gap — that's the new
bottleneck: the *review*, the *operations*, the *security*. The part where
somebody has to decide whether the thing that was just generated is any
good.

And what can the agents not do — ever? Voss again, his words:

"[QUOTE] There is a sense of what good means... that is what the agents
don't have yet."
— Laurie Voss, October 2026

The judgment. The taste. The *spec*. The definition of "good" for a
problem that has never been solved before. That's not a typing problem.
That's a *human knowing what's worth doing* problem.

So here's the question chain that follows — and follow it, because it
leads somewhere specific.

If the writing is nearly free, then what's scarce is the judgment.

If the judgment is scarce, then who gets to exercise it — who gets to
*point* the machines at problems — determines everything that gets built.

And if pointing the machines is the power... then the real question was
never "who pays for AI."

It's "who points it."

[BEAT T7 · THE WITNESSES ON THE PRESENT · 11:30]

Right now, here's how the pointing works.

A handful of labs — with the biggest compute pools — point their machines
at their priorities. Their research. Their benchmarks. Their roadmaps.
That's not a conspiracy; it's just what a company is. Companies point
compute at what companies want.

The rest of the world gets the subscription. Thirty dollars and a queue.

And the people with the judgment — the mathematician with the year of
unpublished work, the maintainer holding a project together for a decade,
the modder with the character rig the community actually wants — they rent
access. They beg for API credits. They build in public and hope the
algorithm is kind.

If you think I'm exaggerating, it happened this month. An indie studio —
Drunken Goddess Reflux — cancelled their own public demo entirely. Not
because the game failed. Because vibe-coding had made it trivial for
other people to copy the concepts, art, and mechanics, and rebuild it on
Roblox within days. The demo — the hundred-year-old ritual where creators
let the world taste the work — is dying, because when your art is
clonable overnight, showing anything is a risk.

The system as it stands punishes the people who show their work... and
rewards whoever has the biggest pile of compute to clone faster.

[PAUSE]

[BEAT T8 · THE HONEST LIMITS · 13:30]

Now. Here's the part a lot of videos would skip, and I won't.

We don't know the full picture either. Nobody can measure yet how much of
the world's electricity AI really uses — the honest scientists say it
plainly, and the honest answer is that net effects are not measurable yet.
Nobody knows how much of any given discovery "belongs" to the humans who
preceded it and the compute that finished it — there is no principled
percentage, and any company that sells you one is selling something.

And a platform like ours cannot fix the whole world, cannot promise
returns, cannot replace the law. The law still governs copies. Courts
still exist — and the way the mathematics dispute played out, with public
statements doing the work of what should have been transparent data, is
exactly what happens when systems don't record the truth as they go.

What we're proposing isn't justice for the past. It's plumbing for the
future.

[BEAT T6-II · THE TURN: WHAT WOULD IT LOOK LIKE · 14:50]

Because here's the part of the story where you — the person who is
building, or wanting to build, or just curious — get to stop renting.

Imagine opening a platform where compute is gathered from many people —
not one company's vault but a commons, pooled and pointed. Where you, or
anyone, can fund a question that matters to them: the modder's character,
the nurse's research run, the engineer's open-source tool that thousands
of people depend on and nobody pays for.

Where a claim is made against a real question — not a pitch deck — and the
compute is released against that claim with hard limits. Where every
milestone leaves a receipt: what was attempted, what was computed, what it
cost, what came out — timestamped, checksummed, public.

Where whoever picks up your idea later — a fork, a remix, a single receipt
— doesn't just take it. The flow routes back. If your work gets used, you
get credited and paid, automatically, because the platform governs the
*flow* and the license governs the *copy*. Open source stops being
charity. It becomes infrastructure that pays.

That's the part the subscription can never give you. Not cheaper compute —
*pointing power*. The right to decide what your twenty dollars — and your
questions — aim the world's machines at... and to own the record of what
you did with them.

Paul Graham wrote this twenty years ago, and it still holds:

"[QUOTE] Actually, startups take off because the founders make them take
off."
— Paul Graham, "Do Things That Don't Scale," July 2013

The tools now let machines do the scaling. The founders — the stewards —
still have to make it take off. Which means what the world needs is not
more compute hoarders. It's more people *pointing* compute — and a system
that remembers they did.

[BEAT T9 · THE STEELMAN + THE CLOSE · 17:00]

Let me steelman the other side before I close, because fairness is the
whole point.

The labs built these models. They spent billions. They owe their
shareholders, they employ thousands, and the subscription pricing is — for
what it gives you — a genuinely good deal. And a commons has real costs:
electricity, cooling, moderation, the legal walls. None of this is free.
The honest case for the way things are: it works, it's getting cheaper,
and the market is doing what markets do.

All true. And none of it changes the gap.

Because the market handles *price* beautifully and *direction* terribly.
The market asks "what's profitable?" — and the world asks "what's
*missing?*" There are questions with no customer. Research no one funds.
Mods no one buys. Cures with markets too small for the tower to notice.
The people closest to those questions — the nurse, the modder, the
lone-wolf scientist, the kid with a game engine idea — have never had a
place to point the fire.

That's the gap. And the whole pitch of Compute Fund is one sentence:

*the people who pay decide.* Not a CEO. Not a committee. The people.

So here's the invitation, and it's the same one the mathematicians never
got: put your name on your work, in a place that records it. Point compute
at the question that keeps you up. Fund the person whose work you use, so
the taking and the giving-back happen in the same motion. And when
somebody asks what you're building — the receipts will answer for you.

Somebody is already paying for AI.

The question that matters is whether the person paying... finally gets to
point.

Compute Fund. The fire, to the commons.

[END — 17:40]
