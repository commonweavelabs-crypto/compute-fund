COMPUTE FUND — GAMING: WHAT MAKES A GREAT PROJECT
(Narrator video script v1 — conversational, ~4.5 min)

[SECTION 1 — WHAT MAKES A GREAT PROJECT]

Every great Compute Fund project starts with one sentence that can be proven right or
wrong. "Given this console's binary, does applying this decompilation recipe produce a
running port on modern hardware?" That's the whole standard. Not a dream. A checkpoint.

A great project also carries three receipts: where the work lives — a public repo we
can verify; what gets delivered — the artifact a stranger can download and inspect;
and what would prove you wrong — the observation that ends the debate. If a stranger
can check those three things, patrons can fund you with confidence, because the
platform shows every run, every token, every receipt — on the record, by default.

[SECTION 2 — THE LAW, PLAIN]

Now the part everyone asks about. Is rebuilding a game legal?

Here's the line the courts drew, decades before AI made it loud. Copyright protects
expression — the code as written, the art, the music, the characters, the names.
It does not protect functionality. That's United States law, Section 102(b): ideas,
procedures and methods are not copyrightable.

Two court cases made this real. Sega versus Accolade: reverse engineering, done the
clean way, is legitimate fair use. Sony versus Connectix: studying a console's
behavior — without copying its code — produced a lawful product. Clean-room means
one team studies the behavior and writes down what it does; a different team
implements from those notes, and the two never swap code.

So on Compute Fund: rebuilding how a game works — decompilations, recompilations,
ports, compatibility layers — is fair game. What is not fair game: ripped textures,
ripped models, ripped music, someone else's characters and trademarks. A legally
perfect recomp with stolen art is still a violation. In 2026 the AI tools got fast
enough that Anthropic itself had to tell Claude Code: build original games, build
genre games, build from your own designs — don't recreate a specific existing game.
We agree with that line, and we go one step friendlier: name the game, describe the
mechanic you love, build YOUR version of it — and list it here.

And one more thing people get wrong: inside Compute Fund you never copy someone's
project — you fork it. A fork is public, credited, and receipted, so the original
author's line stays visible in the ledger forever.

[SECTION 3 — HOW IT WORKS ON THE PLATFORM]

Open a project, and the content agreement comes first — one checkbox that says you
own your rights and you've read the rules. Then the wizard asks for the question,
the artifact, and the budget. When patrons fund it, the gateway dispenses compute
against exactly that budget — allowed models only.

Forking, rent, receipts — here's the honest version. When you fork someone's
project, you take over a working base; the platform records that the original
author's line stays credited, and the fork contributes rent back to the maintainers
who built the base — capped by the platform, public on the ledger. LLM tokens work
the same way: funding a project issues governed compute against it, and every run
posts a receipt — what model, what cost, what came out. No black boxes. If a project
goes quiet after taking money, the platform marks it ready for forking. That's the
abandonment rule: wanted work keeps moving; parked work becomes public opportunity.

[SECTION 4 — OUR GAMING SHELF, REAL EXAMPLES]

Look at the gaming shelf right now. Veloren — a fully open-source multiplayer
world, hundreds of contributors. Shattered Pixel Dungeon — the roguelike that taught
half of indie games how it's done. VKQuake2 — the Quake II port that runs natively
on Nintendo hardware. The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom PC ports — the
total-conversion recomp project. Each one is claimed or claimable, each one posts
receipts, each one keeps its license.

[SECTION 5 — THE CLOSE]

So: build original games. Rebuild the mechanics you love, clean-room, with your own
art. Fork what's open. Prove your milestones. And let the platform carry the trust
for you — every claim verified, every run receipted, every token accounted.

That's Compute Fund. Open a project — the question is the product. We'll see your
name in the ledger.
