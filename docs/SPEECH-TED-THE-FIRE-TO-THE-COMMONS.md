# THE FIRE, TO THE COMMONS
### A TED Talk — Gui Saguia, founder of Compute Fund
*Draft v0 — speech-writer draft, 2026-10-22, from the mission night.
~15 minutes at speaking pace. Written to be memorized and performed.
Stage directions in brackets; everything else is spoken word.*

---

**[He walks out slowly. No slides. One light. He speaks quietly.]**

I want to take you somewhere before I tell you what I built.

Every one of you has felt a version of this feeling. You're playing a game.
A mage walks onto the field — Lulu, in Final Fantasy Ten. She raises her
arms to the sky. She brings them down.

And lightning falls.

Not on her. Not on her enemies. *From her.* She controls the storm. And in
that moment, holding the controller, you feel it — that specific shiver:
*what would it be like to hold that kind of power?*

Now keep that feeling. Because ten thousand years ago, someone felt it for
real.

It's night. It's cold. Everything your people have ever known happens in
the dark. And then there is fire in human hands for the very first time —
warmth that doesn't come from a summer that's ending. Light that doesn't
beg the moon for permission. A tool that turns meat into food, clay into
pottery, night into morning.

Have you ever imagined what that felt like? *A gift straight from the
gods.* Because that's exactly what they thought it was.

The Greeks told a story about how we got it. Prometheus — his name means
"forethought" — went up to the gods and stole fire, and brought it down to
humans. The gods punished him for it. Chained to a rock, forever. Because
the gods understood something we've forgotten:

Fire was never just warmth.

Fire was **deciding**. Whoever holds the fire decides what gets cooked,
what gets forged, what gets built, what gets discovered. The gods weren't
afraid of our warmth. They were afraid of our *aims*.

And then there's a second story we tell even less often. After the theft,
a god went to work. Hephaestus — the god of the forge. Here's what you
need to know about him: the other gods laughed at him. He walked with a
limp. He wasn't invited to the table. So he did something no one expected.

He *built*.

He built talking automatons out of gold. He built walking tripods that
rolled themselves to Olympus. He built armor for heroes and arms for
queens. The Greeks invented the robot in his forge — the maker-god nobody
invited, building the future anyway.

Two myths. One lesson.

**Fire in the hands of the few lights a palace. Fire in the hands of the
many lights a civilization.**

**[Beat. He steps forward.]**

Now I need to tell you about the new fire.

There is a resource on this Earth today more important than oil ever was,
more important than gold ever dreamed of being. It's the resource that
renders your dreams onto screens, and searches the protein folding space
for medicines, and — just weeks ago — shook a mathematics problem that
stood for a century, in eighty-eight hours, with ten thousand reasoning
agents working in coordination.

It's called compute.

And here is the question that keeps me up at night — the only question,
really: **when this fire points at the future… who decides where it
burns?**

Right now? Almost all of it burns where a handful of hands point it.

And I want to be careful here, because this is not a speech about villains.
The people holding that fire right now — I don't hate them. In some ways,
I *admire* them. But I want you to see their position clearly. Imagine a
man given a genie's lamp that never runs out of wishes. Do you really want
*all* the gifts the genie can give? They can give you everything, and it
can go wrong *very* fast — they can give you, in an afternoon, the pain
that was never meant for human hands. That man is *alone* with that lamp.
Every wish he makes, he makes for eight billion people, *alone*.

That's not a throne. That's a burden.

Old myths say punishment is being chained to a rock. I say there's a
modern punishment we never named: **being the only one awake while
everyone sleeps, holding all the choices, with no one to set them down
in front of.**

So the old question answers itself. Not "how do we take the fire back from
them." That's the wrong sentence. The right one is:

**How do we take the fire *off* them — so they can finally put it down?**

**[Beat.]**

Let me show you two futures. Not utopias — *options*.

**Future one. The Tower.**

The fire concentrates. It always does, if nobody builds a counterweight —
concentration is what fire *does*. The Tower decides which diseases get
researched. The Tower decides which ideas are worth one more run of the
models, and which die quietly in someone's notebook. And here's the part
nobody says out loud: the Tower means *waste*. Not evil — waste. For every
dream the Tower funds, ten thousand go unfunded — the nurse with a
research question, the teenager whose game engine could be brilliant, the
family waiting on a cure for something rare. Those people aren't opposed
by anyone. They're just… *unhearable*. One throat, however good, cannot
hear a species.

We are already living a soft version of that future. The question is not
whether we'll drift into it. The question is whether we extend it.

**Future two. A village of fires.**

Picture it honestly — not heaven, just *earthier*. Compute that anyone can
point. A nurse points some at her question. A modder, at the character
that should exist in his favorite game. A lone-wolf scientist, at the
experiment no committee would prioritize. Most of these fires burn small.
Some go out. That's fine — that's what trials are.

But some catch. And here's what's different about the village: when a fire
catches, you can see *exactly* how it caught — because everything leaves a
receipt. When something works, the next person can build on it. When
someone forked an idea and carried it somewhere new, the originator keeps
earning from it — the lineage pays rent, automatically, to the people who
lit the first flame. The person who keeps a project alive for twenty years
isn't a volunteer. They're a **steward** — and a steward can make a
living. Your work history follows you like a passport. And the record —
all of it, timestamped, checksummed, stamped — outlives every one of us.
Long after we're gone, someone investigating something we did will open
the ledger and read the truth of it, in our own words, next to the
machine's own meter.

That's not a feature list. That's a different **paradigm** of discovery.

**[He lets the word land.]**

So — how do we get there?

I want to sell you this the only honest way: **with receipts.**

We didn't write a whitepaper and a vibe. We built the machinery, and we
broke it on purpose, and we fixed it, and we built it again — smaller,
stricter, tested until something snapped, then rebuilt where it snapped.
The rails exist today. A creator can be funded in minutes instead of
months. A project's origin keeps earning after its children succeed. A
single receipt — one model, one dataset, one answer — can be bought
without buying the whole library. A discovery's credit travels to exactly
the people who carried it, because the receipts — not the company, not the
platform, not a committee — decide where value flows.

Is that everything? No. And I won't pretend it is. That's the deal here:
we show our work, and we show it *early*, with the seams visible. The rest
gets built the same way — one project, one receipt, one steward at a time.

We are not asking you to believe in a promise.

We're asking you to check our homework. *That's* the pitch. We can sell it
with receipts.

**[The light widens. He looks up.]**

So here is my invitation. I am not recruiting you for a war against the
Tower. Towers fall on their own, or they don't — that was never our
lever. I'm inviting you to a **village**. To build where building finally
pays. To research where researching finally counts. To cooperate out of
*enlightened self-interest* instead of martyrdom — because in this
village, cooperation is simply the smartest thing to do.

We are here for each other. That was always the whole trick. Every
civilization we've ever admired was just people who decided that the fire
should be shared, and then did the enormous, unglamorous work of sharing
it well.

So — join our platform. Join our dream.

**Give the power of discovery back to the masses.**

The fire from the gods — to the commons.

**[Quiet.]**

Amen.

**[Blackout.]**

### Performance notes (Gui's copy)
- The Lulu opening MUST be told from memory, warm, like confessing
  something. It earns the myth section.
- "Burden" is the hinge word of the whole piece — arrive at it slow, leave
  it fast.
- The paradigm line: pause after "different." Let the audience finish the
  thought.
- The receipts section: speed up slightly — this is the credibility
  engine; confidence = tempo.
- Final minute: slow everything down. "Amen" = almost a whisper; hold
  blackout one full second after.
- Alternative title if THE FIRE, TO THE COMMONS isn't chosen: "Who Holds
  the Fire" / "The Burden of Prometheus".
