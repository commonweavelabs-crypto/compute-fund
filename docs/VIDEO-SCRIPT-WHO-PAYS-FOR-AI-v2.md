[CH 1 · (calm, measured) THE SCENE]

September 7th, 2026. It's a few minutes before midnight in New York.

A mathematician sits at his desk and does something that will consume the
next month of his life: he publishes. Not a paper. A statement.

His name is Tristan Buckmaster. NYU. One of the best fluid-dynamicists
alive. And in his statement, he's not claiming a discovery. He's defending
one.

For a year, in secret, he and a collaborator — Levent Alpöge, a
mathematician at Harvard — had been working on the deepest question in
their field: Navier-Stokes. The equations of flowing water. The Millennium
Prize problem. A million dollars for whoever cracks it.

Twelve hours after his statement goes up, OpenAI announces something
staggering of their own.

Twelve hours.

Hold that thought. And hold this question, because it's the only thing
this video is really about:

Somebody is already paying for AI — that part is certain. So: who, and
how much? And who gets to decide where it points?

[CH 2 · (measured) THE MIRROR]

Maybe you love where this is going. Maybe it frightens you. Most people
— if you're honest — are standing somewhere in the middle: building with
it at two in the morning, worried about it at three.

That's who this video is for: the person in the middle. I'm not here to
move you to a camp. I'm here to follow one ledger line all the way down,
and then show you the other version of the story — the one that doesn't
exist yet. The one you could build.

[CH 3 · (measured) HOW ITS PAID FOR TODAY]

Start with how AI is paid for right now. Because most people have never
seen the whole shape of it.

If you use AI today, you're on a plan. Ten dollars. Twenty. Free, if you
don't mind the queue. Small metered sips of a supercomputer, priced like
a coffee.

And listen — for what it's for, it's a good deal. Sam Altman, OpenAI's
CEO, wrote in January 2025, quote: "The cost to use a given level of AI
falls about 10x every 12 months... the price per token dropped about 150x."
The intelligence is genuinely collapsing in price.

But every pricing page hides the same thing. Your twenty dollars doesn't
buy compute. It buys a place in THEIR queue. Their priority. Their
roadmap. The frontier problems — the ones that take ten thousand
coordinated agents running for days — were never on your menu at any
price.

The pools are huge and few. The pointing happens behind glass.

[CH 4 · (contemplative) THE YEAR OF SECRET WORK]

Back to our mathematicians. Because before anything leaked, before any
announcement — there was a year of quiet, and there was an email.

September 19th, 2025. Alpöge writes to Buckmaster. He predicts — a full
year ahead of it happening — that Navier-Stokes will be the first
Millennium problem solved in their lifetime. And he names the thing he's
afraid of. Quote, from that email: "corporations would claim the deepest
pure math work."

A year later, it's the exact world they're standing in. Their recent
advances, published August 15th — verified in software — get leaked to
OpenAI within days, they say. The swarm runs for 88 hours. A proof comes
out. A formal verification on top. The headlines announce a machine has
done what mathematicians couldn't in a century.

[CH 5 · (low, controlled) THE DISPUTE]

What follows is a month of the strangest credit-war footage you'll ever
see — because almost none of it is about money.

OpenAI says, first: we saw none of their work through any means. Buckmaster
says: our advances seeded your run. OpenAI later acknowledges: yes, session
data — theirs — was used, as de-identified training material. Their CEO
reposts his researcher's version. Somebody suggests Buckmaster rewrite the
proof as sole author — and take the million-dollar prize for it, as, quote,
"the closest humans to the problem" — while the method stays theirs. He
declines. Eventually twenty-eight Fields Medalists sign a letter warning
about exactly this: AI companies treating the deepest problems as
benchmarks.

I want to be careful here. There is no court ruling. Nobody has been
found to have done anything illegal. Maybe they didn't.

But watch the shape. A year of human work. A leak. A hundred-million-
dollar compute run. And at the end: whose name goes on the discovery? Who
gets credited? Who gets anything?

The people paid in years. The company paid in compute. And the credit...
settles however the bigger table wants it to.

[CH 6 · (contemplative) THE OTHER VERSION OF THE STORY]

Now imagine the same story, one door over — the version that didn't happen,
but could have.

Same mathematicians. Same year of secret work. Same models — because
they used both labs' tools, that part's true. But this time, the work runs
on a ledger. Every session, every result, every checkpoint: receipted.
Timestamped. Signed. Their August-the-fifteenth result is not a blog post
they have to defend. It's a public record with their signatures already on
it, older than the leak by definition.

In that world, the announcement war can't happen. There's nothing to
dispute. The provenance is not an argument anyone can win — because it
isn't an argument. It's a receipt.

Notice what changed. Not the laws. Not the licenses. The *plumbing*.

Nobody has to remember to give credit when the credit is minted at the
same time as the work.

That's the entire gap between our world and theirs. Our world relies on
goodwill — and goodwill is a virtue, not an economy. Their world runs on
receipts — and receipts are an economy.

[CH 7 · (measured) THE DATA BENEATH YOUR FEET]

And this isn't just about mathematicians. It's happening under everybody
right now.

The man who co-founded npm — Laurie Voss, the registry most of the
world's code flows through — stood on a stage in October 2026 and put
numbers on it. Quote: "Developers who switch on autonomous coding agents
write 741 percent more code — eight times as much. But they ship only 30
percent faster."

Eight times the code. A third more shipping. Where does the difference
go? Into review. Into operations. Into security. Into the one job the
machines can't take: quote, "There is a sense of what good means... that
is what the agents don't have yet."

The judgment. The taste. The deciding-what's-worth-building. That was
always the human part. It's just the only human part now.

Which leads to the question under the question.

[CH 8 · (calm, building) WHO POINTS IT]

Somebody is already paying for AI. We know who: you, in subscriptions;
companies, in billions; the neighbors of every data center, in their
electric bills. That question, we answered.

But paying is only half of power. The other half is pointing. And today,
the pointing belongs to whoever holds the biggest pools.

A few labs, pointing machines at their priorities. Their research. Their
benchmarks. That's not villains — companies point compute at what
companies want. It's not even malice. It's just gravity.

Meanwhile the people with the judgment — the mathematician with a year of
unpublished work, the maintainer carrying a project for a decade, the
modder holding the rig the community wants, the nurse with the research
question, the kid with the game idea — they rent. They wait in queues.
They build in public and hope nothing eats them.

It happened this month, in gaming. An indie studio cancelled their own
public demo — not because the game failed, but because vibe-coding had
made it trivial to clone their art and mechanics and ship a knockoff on
Roblox within days. When your work can be cloned overnight, the only safe
move is to show nothing. The people closest to the questions are being
taught to hide.

[CH 9 · (resolved, steady) THE OTHER DOOR]

Unless the hiding has a door.

Here's the version I want to spend the rest of my life on. A platform
where compute is pooled from many people instead of a few vaults. Where
anyone can fund a question — a real question, stated plainly, like the
CFQ: what do we want to know? Where the machine time is released against
that claim with hard limits, and when the work lands, a receipt exists:
what was attempted, what it cost, what came out. Timestamped. Checksummed.
Signed. Public.

And the part that changes everything downstream: when someone else uses
what you made — forks your project, buys one receipt, stitches your
rigged model into theirs — the value doesn't silently walk away. The flow
routes back. The platform governs the flow. The license governs the copy.
Open work stops being charity. It becomes infrastructure that pays.

That's what the subscription can never sell you. Cheaper compute isn't
the prize. Pointing is the prize. The right to aim your questions — and
the world's surplus machines — at the things only *you* know matter.

Paul Graham wrote it twenty years ago: startups take off because the
founders make them take off. The machines now supply the scaling. The
pointing is still ours. What the future needs — what I'm building — is a
ledger that remembers who pointed, every single time.

[CH 10 · (measured, warm) THE CLOSE]

So let me steelman the system we have before I end this. The labs built
these models. They spent billions. They employ thousands. The
subscription is, for what it gives, a good deal. Electricity is not free.
Cooling is not free. The law still exists, and it still matters. All of
that is true, and none of it is the villain of this story.

Here's what it can't do. The market prices what's profitable. The world
is full of what's *missing* — the cures with markets too small to notice,
the tools no one owns, the questions that belong to a family or a niche
or a neighborhood. Those questions never had a patron, because there was
no place to be one.

Somebody is already paying for AI. Everyone is — in money, in bills, in
uncredited work.

So the question I'll leave you with is the one this whole video has been
walking toward:

Should the person paying finally get to point?

Because the minute they can — the minute there's a ledger where funding,
judgment, and receipts are the same motion — the fire stops being a
hoard, and becomes what it always should have been.

Compute Fund. The fire, to the commons.
